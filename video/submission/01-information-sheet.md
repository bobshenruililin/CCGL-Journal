## Why did your group decide on this topic?

> Draft, about 230 words. Rewrite it in your own voice; the marker will read it as yours.

Most of what we learned in this course came from cases where someone had to make a call without knowing how it would land: a manager deciding whether to give hard feedback, a leader choosing between control and autonomy, a hire made on assumptions. We kept noticing that reading a case is not the same as making the call. You always know the ending.

So we asked one question: **how do you let someone practise leading before it's for real?** Our answer was to build a game where nobody tells you the right answer.

In *Six Flights*, you play Yan, a student who inherits a rooftop film night. Over one week you make three decisions, and each one changes who ends up carrying what. There are 27 possible weeks and four endings. There is no score. The only feedback comes at the end, when the other characters write in your notebook about what your choices were like to live with.

Each of us then took one part of the game and connected it to the course and to our own experience: asking before you assume, how the way you treat people decides who shows up for you, why no leadership style is right every time, giving everyone a part that's theirs, and hearing how you led from the people you led.

We also learned leadership by doing it. Four of us, plus a lot of tools, had to share one vision, divide the work and give each other honest feedback. The video is our reflection on both.

**How we built it (evidence of process)**
- **Three builds, one question.** Team Quest scored leadership with meters. Playing it made us ask who actually pays for a leader's choices, which became Six Flights. Asterwind then put the player face to face with that person.
- **27 routes, 4 endings, no score.** Every combination of the three decisions produces a different week, and automated tests check that no single choice wins on every route.
- **Every word is authored**, about 6,000 words of story. Consequences come from other characters' handwriting, not from a narrator.
- **Art direction.** Over 300 painted studies and 8 full-size alternatives were reviewed; about 40 painted images made it into the game.
- **Accessible.** Keyboard play, a plain-type mode, a reduced-motion mode and a phone layout.
- **A real division of labour.** Ideas, scripts, art and code moved between people and tools through 14 written briefs, all kept in our repository.
- **The video.** Each member wrote their own reflection; scripts were shaped from those and recorded in our own words.
