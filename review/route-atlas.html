Tuesday · I could fix this. That was the problem.

Eighteen slides. Seven minutes to speak. Three days until we ask the campus panel to look into keeping one study room open later.

Jules has spent a week listening to students. Every slide is full of their words. I can see a cleaner story hiding underneath. I can also see how long it will take to explain it.

“Every time I cut a quote,” Jules says, “it sounds like we’re speaking for them.”

My phone lights up. Dev: “Tomorrow’s rehearsal is still happening, right? I start work at six.”

Chosen line

“Send me the file. I’ll reshape it tonight.”

Protect tomorrow’s rehearsal. Take the edit on yourself.

What follows

By 1:18, the deck has eight slides. The argument is clearer. I’ve made the last train, barely.

In the morning, Jules circles one of my edits. “This person said they’d stay late if the buses still ran. You’ve cut the ‘if’.”

The rehearsal slot is safe. One sentence isn’t. We put the condition back.

Perspective and reflection

Jules: I was relieved when Mina offered. Then I saw the quote. I don’t need every word kept. I need to know which ones are being cut.

Gain: You protected the rehearsal by taking on the structural edit.

Cost: The time came from your sleep. Jules still had to check that the shortened quotations meant the same thing.

Before taking over, what would you agree about what can change—and who gets the final check?

Chosen line

“Check that one thing. We’ll agree a fallback.”

Jules follows up. You and Dev rehearse without them.

What follows

We agree the line before Jules sends the message: demand for this particular room is still unconfirmed.

One reply comes in before the panel. “Maybe, if the late bus stop is close.” Useful. Not a yes.

Dev and I have practised the handover twice. Jules has to learn it on the day.

Perspective and reflection

Jules: We got a better question, not the reassurance I’d hoped for. I’m glad we agreed what we’d say before the reply arrived.

Gain: You put an important assumption to the people affected, with a fallback if they did not reply.

Cost: Jules missed the run-through. One reply would still be too little to establish demand.

What answer would change your plan—and what will you do if no answer arrives?

Friday · Then someone asked about the money.

Jules waits half a beat for the handover. We didn’t rehearse this part together. I move my notes aside so they can see the next line.

A panel member leans forward. “What would the extra staffing cost?”

We haven’t got a verified estimate. Dev has read the room’s opening rules, but none of us knows the staffing rates or who would need to be on duty.

The question hangs there. I feel the familiar urge to make the gap disappear.

Chosen line

Explain the assumptions. Ask for a feasibility review.

Keep the conversation moving while naming what is unknown.

What follows

“We don’t have a staffing estimate,” I say. “The request is a feasibility review of one room for two weeks, including who would need to be there.”

The panel member writes something down. “Then put staffing and late transport in the review questions.”

I do. We have a next step to propose, not a promise that the room will open.

Perspective and reflection

Dev: I could hear the difference between not having an answer and trying to hide it. This time we let the question stay a question.

Gain: You made a specific next step possible without inventing a cost.

Cost: The panel could reasonably question your assumptions before supporting a review.

Which assumption would you be willing to have someone challenge in public?

Afterwards

I fixed the deck quickly. I also learned that an edit can change what someone meant. Next time, I want a check before a handover.

We asked a better question and got a conditional reply. I want to remember that evidence can make a plan less tidy and more useful.

I didn’t fill the silence with a number. I made the next question clear.

Outside, Jules’s phone lights up. They turn it over without opening the message. Dev sets the orange timer between us, switched off. We can give each other this minute.
